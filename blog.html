Frequently Asked Questions

Recovery Science FAQs

Evidence-backed answers to common questions regarding cold exposure, compression mechanics, and protocol timing.

How does Whole-Body Cryotherapy differ from ice baths?

Air-based cryotherapy exposes the skin to dry, sub-zero air (-130°C) for 3 minutes without moisture. Because air has lower thermal conductivity than water, it triggers rapid cutaneous thermoreceptor activation and systemic vasoconstriction without drastically lowering deep core muscle temperature. This allows athletes to train again sooner without the prolonged stiffness or delayed re-warming associated with ice baths.

When is the optimal timing window for compression therapy?

Clinical data indicates the optimal window is within 30 to 90 minutes post-exertion. Dynamic sequential compression mimics the body's natural muscle pump, accelerating venous return and lymphatic drainage to clear metabolic byproducts before muscle soreness (DOMS) and cellular swelling set in. Sessions on scheduled rest days are equally effective for flushing residual fatigue.

Will cold therapy blunt muscle hypertrophy or strength gains?

Extreme cold exposure immediately following dedicated hypertrophy training can dampen post-exercise anabolic signaling (specifically the mTOR pathway). If muscular growth is your primary adaptation goal, we recommend delaying cryotherapy by at least 4–6 hours post-lifting, or scheduling sessions on conditioning, endurance, or active recovery days.

How do Infrared Saunas stimulate heat shock proteins?

Unlike traditional saunas that heat ambient air to 80–100°C, full-spectrum infrared saunas emit light wavelengths (near, mid, and far) that penetrate 3–4 cm deep into joint and muscle tissue at gentler ambient temperatures (45–65°C). This direct cellular hyperthermia stimulates heat shock protein (HSP70) synthesis, enhances mitochondrial output, and aids tissue repair with lower cardiovascular stress.
